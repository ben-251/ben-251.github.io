Elizabeth Bishop looks outward, closely inspecting the world and its details, yet it is precisely this attention that allows her to indirectly portray human experience. The main way this happens is through the speakers of her poems, who are looking into the world. 

How does she portray the minds of animals, and of speakers interacting with them?

# The Eye
For Bishop, to be a mind thinking, means looking attentively at all the world’s detail, which in turn requires healthy eyes. It is interesting, then, that Bishop chooses to portray several of her animal characters as having damaged or imperfect eyes. 

In *The Fish*, our speaker examines the eyes of a catch:

> …the irises backed and packed  
> with tarnished tinfoil  
> seen through the lenses  
> of old scratched isinglass.  
> They shifted a little, but not  
> to return my stare.  
> —It was more like the tipping  
> of an object toward the light.
> <cite>The Complete Poems, p.43</cite>
{.poem}

Much of the language here is pessimistic about the fish’s ability to see the world meaningfully. The constrained assonance of ‘backed and packed’ and the gross image of ‘old scratched isinglass’ are just some of the ways the fish’s sight is undermined. This last one is particularly horrific because isinglass is a material formed from a fish’s bladders; it is as though the fish’s eyes have been grafted in. His eyes are as useless for looking as they are revolting to look at. He does not look at anything, let alone examine it. His eyes are ‘object[s]’ rather than organs. This is an animal whose eyes are much more like glass eyes, ornamental and representative objects, than “windows to the soul.”[^1]

*Roosters* contains a similarly pessimistic portrayal:

> where in the blue blur   
> their rustling wives admire,  
> the roosters brace their cruel feet and glare   
> 
> with stupid eyes   
> while from their beaks there rise       
> the uncontrolled, traditional cries.  
> <cite>The Complete Poems, p.35</cite> 
{.poem}

The poem is a slow pan through a farm, exploring the character of the roosters and hens. From the first stanza we were introduced to the sound the hens make, developed as an uncivilised, animal pattern, rather than any meaningful communication. Even worse than their ‘horrible insistence’, however, is the chickens’ inability to look *at* anything. We read “their rustling wives admire”, but we don’t know what they are admiring! Instead, we proceed straight into the next line, where “the roosters … glare // with stupid eyes…” Again, this does not conclude with the object of the roosters’ glaring. The reader must begrudgingly accept the speaker’s choice to leave this unresolved, diving right back to the crowing of the roosters. It is as if any attempt by the chickens to reason, to see, to think, is blocked by their instinct to make noises. 

Not every animal is quite this stupid, however. In *Rainy Season; Sub-Tropics*[^2], three distinct personas (a toad, crab, and snail) interact with each other in a single scene, taking turns to narrate their perspectives. The very structure of this poem reveals a sense of perception by the animals, and might tempt us into thinking the animals are capable of seeing fully, like the attentive speaker. This, I fear, is one step too optimistic. For a start, all the animals are limited in their perspectives. The toad’s eyes “bulge and hurt”, and though they “see too much”, they don’t see what it wishes to see most, that is, the droplets on its back. So much of what Bishop calls for through her craft is for the poet to look at the details. It is not enough to simply take everything in, but you should focus on something in particular and examine that. The toad has a focus, a desire, but cannot carry it out, thus wasting his eyesight. Later, we will see that these animals make incredibly flawed judgements, shown to us through clever uses of irony across the three sections. 
{.para-break}

The strongest candidates for attentive animals are found in *The Sandpiper* and *The Moose.* The former follows a frantic bird on the beach, searching for ‘something, something, something.’ From this alone we get a sense of the bird’s insistence. Like the toad, for whom there is “not much to see”, the sandpiper is not interested in the large-scale world, taking it “for granted”. However, unlike the self-obsessed tropical animals, the sandpiper *is* paying close attention to elements of his world. While he does this, the speaker shifts her perspective[^3] continually, attempting to accurately depict *his* perspective:

> He runs, he runs \[…] watching his toes.
>  <cite>The Complete Poems, p.131</cite>
{.poem}

> —Watching, rather, the spaces of sand between them,
>  <cite>Ibid.</cite>
 {.poem}
 
> …As he runs,   
> he stares at the dragging drains.   
> <cite>Ibid.</cite>
{.poem}

> he stares at the dragging drains.   
> <cite>Ibid.</cite>
{.poem}

Once the speaker makes these corrections, she is fully of one mind with the bird. We never discover what he is really after, but it seems he is redirected by something more precious. Thus the poem closes with the speaker and bird zeroing in on the tiniest of details: 

> His beak is focussed; he is preoccupied,  
>   
>  looking for something, something, something.  
>  Poor bird, he is obsessed!  
>  The millions of grains are black, white, tan, and gray,  
>  mixed with quartz grains, rose and amethyst.
> <cite>Ibid.</cite>
{.poem}

*The Moose* follows a bus ride and its eventual interruption by a moose on the road. The lines of importance come near the end of the poem:

> Taking her time,  
> she looks the bus over,  
> grand, otherworldly.  
> Why, why do we feel  
> (we all feel) this sweet  
> sensation of joy?  
> <cite>The Complete Poems, p.173</cite>
{.poem}

The moose here does two things the sandpiper did not: she is slow, and she has a clear object of attention. The speaker of the poem has led us on precisely this journey, delaying the interaction between bus and moose by twenty-one stanzas, spending all that time instead on the bus, the people in it, and the landscapes they travel through. It is for this reason that the phrase “looks the bus over” is all we need to understand the perspective of the moose. We have through on the exact process that the moose is now undertaking. Satisfyingly, the phrase “grand, otherworldly” can modify either the moose or the bus (in the moose’s perspective). The moose is grand and otherworldly to the humans, experiencing the world in a completely different way; and the bus is grand and otherworldly to the moose, a large interruption to her natural world. 

In many ways, the moose sees things the humans could not. The humans were trapped inside their mechanical makeshift home, sleeping and getting cozy, while the moose lives outside, in the natural world. The sensation of joy the passengers feel surely is tied to the complete contrast between the way the moose lives and the way they do. The moose is not focused on sorting through events of the past, through “deaths, deaths and sicknesses”. The moose is interested in the presence of the bus, and now the passengers are interested in the presence of the moose.

It may seem that Bishop portrays animals as inattentive and violent in order to raise humans up as the superior beings, but I believe a more reasonable conclusion is that the default state of humans is quite like her animals. Most of human experience is ignorance, vanity, self-obsession, and a struggle for power, and only those who choose to pause, to look at the world closely, are capable of truly finding beauty and peace.
